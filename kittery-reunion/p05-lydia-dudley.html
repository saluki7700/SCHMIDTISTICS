Schmidtistics · Kittery, September 2026

Lydia Jane Dudley

York and Kittery, Maine, and Lynn, Massachusetts · born about 6 May 1819 · died 24 September 1894

Her husband cannot be found in the deed record of York County. She is in it for thirty-one years.

That is not a figure of speech. The registry's Dudley grantor block was read in full, and the grantee block after it, across a hundred and twenty-five years — 1760 to 1885 — and John H. Dudley is not in either. He never bought, sold or mortgaged land in the county where he lived his whole life.

Lydia did. Repeatedly, in her own name, while she was married to him.

The thing that fell out of looking for his signature

In August 2026 this project set out to do something small and technical: compare a witness signature reading "John Dudley" on somebody else's 1862 deed against a known John H. Dudley hand, to see whether it was our man.

The search for a known hand came back empty, and the reason it came back empty is the whole shape of this marriage.

Every Dudley instrument in the register was checked. The 1855 York deed and the 1864 York mortgage have Lydia as the party taking title — and a grantee signs nothing. The 1882 and 1883 Kittery deeds are after John was dead. That left exactly one instrument in the world in which he could have signed anything at all.

⭐ There is no known signature of John H. Dudley because this family's real estate was held by the wife. The negative test produced a positive finding about her.

(The comparison was abandoned on its own merits too: York County deed books are record copies, and every "signature" in them is the Register's handwriting, not the signer's. No handwriting comparison between two recorded deeds can prove anything about either man.)

A birthday computed from the day she died

No birth entry has ever been found for her. The town of York has been searched across eight spellings of the surname and it is not there.

But her Massachusetts death return gives her age to the day — 75 years, 4 months, 18 days — against a death of 24 September 1894. That arithmetic runs back to about 6 May 1819.

⚑ Treat it as a target, not a fact. A computed date is only ever as good as the age the informant gave, and the same informant could not name her parents. But the project had carried nothing better than "b. abt 1819" from census ages, and it had never had a day and a month. A York birth entry for a Voudy child of early May 1819 would be her.

Entered June 3d, 1840

The marriage is in the York town book, and it took this project weeks of looking in Kittery, in Portsmouth and in the indexes before anyone looked in the York publishments where it had been all along:

Mr. John Dudley a resident in York and Miss Lydia Jane Voudy of York intend marriage. Entered June 3d. 1840. Certificate issued June 17, 1840.**

She was twenty-one. He had been home from the Antarctic for nine years.

Then the census pages, which are the only place the household can be seen whole:

  • York, 1850 — John 40, sailor, real estate $200; Lydia 29; Mary 9, John 7, Ann 5, Henrietta 3.
  • York, 1860 — John 48, mariner; Lydia J. 37; Mary C. 18, domestic, born Massachusetts; John W. 16, Ann S. 14, Florence H. 12, Edward K. 3, George W. 1 — and a Jane A. Whittier, twelve, in the house.
  • Kittery, 1870 — John 65, mariner, retired; Lydia J. 50, keeping house; Edward K. 12 at school; George W. 9.
  • Kittery, 1880 — John 73, retired mariner; Lydia J. 59; Edw. K. 21, carpenter; Geo. W. 19, labourer.

Six children across twenty years, and the household's real estate never rises above two hundred dollars in the column where the census asks about it. That column asks the head of the household. It was not where this family's property was.

1855: one hundred and eighty-five dollars

On 2 August 1855, recorded two days later at Book 241 page 146, Joanna S. Douglass of York conveyed to Lydia Jane Dudley — named in the deed as "wife of John Dudley" — a half-acre in York with a dwelling house on it, for one hundred and eighty-five dollars.

The bounds are the kind of thing a town remembers itself by:

south-easterly by the road leading from the Court House in York to Dennett's Wharf; south-westerly by the road from the dwelling house of the late Thomas B. Clark; north-westerly as now fenced; north-easterly by Meeting House Creek.

Witnessed by Abbie P. Junkins and Jno. P. Junkins, acknowledged before Jno. P. Junkins, Justice of the Peace, and entered by Zenas Shaw Junior, Register.

⭐ This is the earliest document in which she holds anything, and it is eighteen years earlier than the purchase this family already knew about. She was thirty-six. Her eldest was fourteen. It is the house the 1860 census finds them in.

And look at how the land got to her

The half-acre did not come to Lydia in one hop. On 25 July 1855 the Douglasses — Joanna S., Franklin J. and Samuel M. — conveyed it to Nathaniel M. Marshall. On 2 August, Marshall conveyed it back to Joanna S. Douglass alone. And on the same 2 August, Joanna S. Douglass sold it to Lydia Jane Dudley.

⭐⭐ That out-and-back is not an accident, and it is not the first time this seller did it. Seven years earlier, on 13 March 1848, two deeds were written on one opening of Book 200: Samuel A. Douglass and Joanna S. his wife conveyed about a hundred acres of York pasture — the Bean Pasture and the Connecticut Places — to George Moulton Junior for eight hundred dollars; and the very next instrument on the leaf is George Moulton Junior conveying the identical land, for the identical eight hundred dollars, to Joanna S. Douglass alone.

No money moved and no land changed hands. What changed is whose name it stood in.

⚑ So the woman who sold Lydia her first house had already put a hundred acres into her own sole name by exactly the manoeuvre the little half-acre went through on its way to Lydia. A married woman holding the family land in her own name was a thing done in that town, in that generation, by women who knew each other. It was not a peculiarity of Lydia's — which makes the pattern in her own deeds less surprising and rather more deliberate.

1864: she sells it, and then she lends

Nine years later the family left York for Kittery, and the York house was sold — by her.

At Book 289 page 196, Lydia Jane Dudley of Kittery, still styled "wife of John Dudley", conveyed the half-acre to Joanna C. Raynes of York, single woman, for one hundred and twenty-five dollars. The description is word for word the parcel she had bought in 1855: the road to Sewall's Bridge on one side, the road by Jeremiah McIntire's house on another, the creek on the third, "with the dwelling house and out buildings on the same."

And then, on 29 October 1864, at Book 290 page 232, the same Joanna C. Raynes gave a mortgage back to Lydia — sixty-two dollars, with a defeasance clause voiding it if the note were repaid with interest inside a year.

⭐⭐ Read the two together and the transaction is plain: Lydia sold for a hundred and twenty-five and carried sixty-two of it herself. One woman selling a house to another woman and financing half the price. She is not the borrower in this family's papers. She is the lender.

The mortgage was witnessed by Alex Dennett and John H. Locke and acknowledged before Dennett as Justice of the Peace. Two twenty-five-cent Internal Revenue stamps are on the leaf, and a vertical marginal annotation that looks like a discharge and has not been read.

The thousand-dollar acre

On 31 March 1873 she bought again, and this time for real money: one thousand dollars, paid to John E. Lewis of Kittery for a lot with the buildings on it, running to "the road leading from Kittery Foreside to the Nursery of John Rogers." The deed calls her a married woman and conveys to her "her heirs and assigns forever."

A thousand dollars is the largest sum anywhere in this family's papers. And because the acre was hers and not his, it was never in John H. Dudley's estate when he died — which is why no inventory of it exists, and why his widow and sons could convey it themselves afterwards rather than waiting on an administrator.

The fire

On the morning of 28 October 1880 the house burned.

"The dwelling house at Kittery, owned by Lydia J. Dudley, together with a portion of the contents, was burned out this morning. Loss about $1500; partially insured. Cause, defective chimney."

Three papers carried it — the Boston Traveler, the Boston Evening Transcript and, the next day, the Springfield Daily Union. All three name the owner. All three name her. Not one of them mentions a husband, and John H. Dudley was alive and living in it.

He was seventy-four. He died ten and a half months later, on 15 September 1881.

⚑ And a thing that must be flagged rather than asserted: "together with a portion of the contents." This project has repeatedly found John H. Dudley to be thinner in the record than any comparable man — no birth entry, no deed, no probate, no death return. Some of that is structural. A house fire that took part of the contents is a candidate explanation for why nothing descended in the family either. It is speculation. It is also the kind of thing that belongs in the story rather than in a footnote.

1882: reserving the sole use and improvement to myself

Ten months after she was widowed, on 1 August 1882, Lydia J. Dudley of Kittery, widow, conveyed the acre to her two sons:

in consideration of love and good will which I have for my sons, as well as one hundred dollars, paid by my sons Edward K. Dudley and George W. Dudley… reserving however, the sole use and Improvement of the above described premises to myself during my natural life.**

This is the direct-line document. It is the deed that says in so many words that Edward K. and George W. are her sons — and therefore the brothers of Mary Catherine, who had married a German immigrant named Schmidt at York in 1861. Everything after this family in this archive runs through that sentence.

Two further things sit inside it. She is styled widow, which fixes John's death from the land record alone, with no death record needed. And she signed her own name. The witnesses were Alexander Dennett and a Henrietta F. Marat — her own daughter. That is the Florence H. Dudley who is twelve years old on the 1860 York page; who married Albert S. Chew in 1871; who married Melville R. Muratt at Portsmouth on 29 August 1876, on a record that names her parents John and Lydia J. D. in so many words; and who died at Lynn in 1923 as Henrietta Florence Murat, still naming John Dudley as her father. ⚑ The deed uses no relationship word — but the woman holding the pen over her mother's signature is documented twice over, in two states, forty-seven years apart.

⭐ The life estate is not a lawyer's flourish; it is a family habit. The same reservation — the fee to the next generation, the use kept back for life — is the exact device used in the 1848 deed of William Voudy, who conveyed his nine-acre York farm to his son Reuben while reserving it "during my life and her life" for himself and his wife Dorcas. That is the man this project believes to be Lydia's father, though as the last section says, no document says so.

The following April she and the two young men sold the burnt-out lot to Andrew P. Wentworth for two hundred and fifty dollars. A thousand in 1873; two-fifty in 1883. A fire and a death is what happened in between.

Wyman Street

And then, at sixty-seven, she turns up in Massachusetts as a landowner.

On 23 October 1886 the Board of Mayor and Aldermen of the City of Lynn gave notice that they intended to lay out and accept Wyman Street, from Boston Street to Walnut Street, and named the parties interested so they could object:

Bridget Cunningham, Daniel Mahoney, John E. Elliot, Daniel Reardon, Lydia J. Dudley, Oliver S. Roberts, Isaac C. Wyman, Michael Shanahan, Mather H. Holmes, John P. Leavitt, Eaton W. Lothrop, Martin Coyne, Samuel B. Hussey, Marshall H. Abbott, Martha Green, and all other persons or parties interested…

The hearing was set for eight in the evening on Tuesday 16 November at the City Hall, and the notice signed by Charles E. Parsons, City Clerk.

⭐ A layout notice names the abutters. So she held land on Wyman Street in 1886 — five years after her husband died, and eight years before she died at 101 Wyman Street. She was an owner in Lynn, not a widow taken in by her children.

The scattering to Essex County follows the fire and the death rather than any earlier drift: Florence Henrietta was at Lynn from about 1883, George W. married there in 1889, Mary was there by 1900. Lydia appears to have gone there and bought.

What the death return would not say

She died on 24 September 1894 at 101 Wyman Street, Lynn, of peritonitis, aged seventy-five. The funeral notice ran in the local paper. She was brought back to Kittery and buried with John, under a stone that carries both their names.

The Massachusetts return — volume 445, page 505, entry 741 — is a printed form with a column block headed Names and birthplaces of parents, and it is the single piece of paper that should have closed the oldest open question in this archive.

It has been read at the image. The clerk left the names blank. The lines immediately above and below hers carry parents in that same block, so the column was being filled that day; it was left empty for her.

But the birthplace half of the block is not empty. It reads "Me." and "Me." — both of them.

⚑ So whoever reported her death knew where each of her parents had been born, said so, and could not say what they were called. That rules out any parentage from outside Maine. It fits William Voudy and Dorcas Booker of York exactly — and it fits a great many other people just as well. It is corroboration of place, not an identification, and this document is never going to give a name.

What is not established

  • Her parentage. No document states that Lydia Jane Voudy was the daughter of William Voudy and Dorcas Booker. It is a strong inference from town, generation and family memory — and the entire Voudy, Varrell, Sadler and Isles of Shoals ancestry hangs from it. Her 1894 death return was opened and does not close it.
  • Her birth date and place. About 6 May 1819 is arithmetic off a death return, not a record. York has been searched across eight spellings of the surname and has no birth entry for her.
  • Where the money came from. The deeds record who took the title, which is a different question. Whether the $185 of 1855 or the $1,000 of 1873 was her own money, her husband's, or her family's is not traceable in anything this project holds.
  • An 1889 deed at Lynn in the name of a Lydia J. Dudley has been seen. That she is our Lydia is probable and is not confirmed, and it must not be carried as though it were.
  • What she owned on Wyman Street. The 1886 notice proves she abutted the street. No Essex County deed to her has yet been pulled, and that is an entirely open trail.
  • The marginal annotation on the 1864 mortgage, which appears to be a discharge, and the two revenue stamps beside it. Not read.
  • Whether her Kittery house stood on the acre she bought in 1873. The fire report gives no street, and a local Portsmouth or Kittery paper of late October 1880 would very likely name it. Not yet searched.

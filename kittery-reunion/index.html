Three towns and a stretch of water

Stand on the bridge at Kittery and you can see most of it at once. York up the coast, Kittery under your feet, Portsmouth across the river, and out past the harbour mouth on a clear day, low on the horizon, the Isles of Shoals.

This family has been inside that view for well over three hundred years.

The earliest of us the documents can actually reach is Philip Adams, and we meet him only because he was already dead. On 10 December 1694 his widow sold a piece of land at York, and the deed opens by naming her:

Matthew Austin of York and Elizabeth Adams, relict and administratrix unto Phillip Adams late of York deceased…

Six pounds in current money of New England, paid by Joannah Livingstone of York. Nine generations stand between that sentence and the grandparents most of us remember, and every one of them in between has been checked.

What happened in between happened almost entirely within about fifteen miles.

The names are these. Booker and Moore and Adams at York, farming and fishing, back into the 1600s. Voudy, Varrell and Sadler out on the Isles of Shoals, where a town called Gosport sat on a bare rock nine miles at sea and this family helped run it — their births entered in the same book, on the same page, as the Muchemores and the Shapleys, and their daughters marrying Curriers and Walpeys. Ellingwood at York. Dudley at Kittery. Tucker later. And, arriving very late and from a very long way off, Schmidt.

⚠ One name that used to sit in that list has been moved out of it: Amee, at Kittery. The Amees are in this story — they are neighbours in the same deeds, and a Caty Amee married a William Dudley at Kittery in 1803 — but the register ruled on 1 September 2026 that the branch is allied by marriage, not a line of descent. No descent from the Amees has been shown, and the reason it was once thought otherwise, together with what the marriage does and does not prove, is set out in John H. Dudley.

And most of them worked on the water.

Read the old deeds and the same word keeps arriving after the men's names, the way a trade follows a surname — mariner, mariner, fisherman, seaman. William Booker, fisherman, selling land in 1760. Solomon Varrell, mariner, selling to John Voudy, mariner, in 1799. Edward Voudy, mariner, conveying to his son William Voudy, mariner, in 1803. Four Amees at Kittery in four deeds, every one of them described by what he did on the water. Boys of seventeen and eighteen on West Indies crew lists, measured and described by a customs officer in case they drowned. A Voudy who owned a quarter of a fishing schooner outright.

One of them went a very long way indeed. John H. Dudley shipped out of Portsmouth in 1829 on a sixty-eight-ton schooner bound for the Cape Verde Islands and then for South Georgia, at the edge of the Antarctic ice, and was gone two years. Half a century later his obituary did not say whose son he was. It said which ship he had been on.

Two of them went to war, eighty-eight years apart.

Aaron Booker walked out of York as a volunteer in the last days of January 1776, aged seventeen, and was standing at Dorchester when the British army evacuated Boston that March. He served twice, never received a written discharge, could not sign his own name, and swore his pension declaration from a sickbed at seventy-three because he was too ill to go to the courthouse.

Edward Alfred Henry Schmidt enlisted in the United States Navy under a false name, rose from landsman to acting master's mate, and in June 1864 volunteered to take a party into Confederate-held territory across a corner of the Dismal Swamp to find out where a rebel ironclad was. He came back with the information and with the illness that ended his career.

And then there are the Schmidts, who do not fit the pattern at all.

Everybody else in this story stayed put for three centuries. The Schmidts crossed the world in three generations.

Prussia → London → Kittery → California

Otto Schmidt was a Prussian schoolmaster in London — "I am not a native of this country, but a Prussian," he wrote of himself in 1842 — who taught languages, published grammars in Berlin and London, and had ten children in twelve years, of whom five were living when he wrote it down.

His son Edward left London alone at eighteen, sailed for New York, went into the American navy, and married Mary Catherine Dudley at York in 1861 — the moment the wandering line and the rooted one meet. Everything on this page above that marriage is hers. Everything above it on his side is in another country.

And Edward's youngest son, William Edward Schmidt, born at Kittery in 1871, went west again — Maine, then Washington, then Kansas, then San Diego, where he died in 1959.

Three hundred years within fifteen miles, and then one family crossing an ocean and a continent inside a hundred.

Where this came from

It started with Great-Grandpa Schmidt.

K.O. Schmidt left the family a set of handwritten notes — names, dates, and the stories he had been told. Some of it was solid. Some of it was family legend, in the way that every family has legend. All of it was a beginning, and about fifteen years ago it became the reason somebody started pulling at the threads.

What grew out of those notes was years of looking: burying grounds and town clerks' offices, registries of deeds, libraries and archives, worked into travels that kept finding one more question behind the last one.

What is on these pages is the stage after that, and it is a different kind of thing. Nothing here rests on the notes, or on a family tree, or on anybody's recollection. Every date, name and quotation traces to a document somebody has actually opened and read — a deed, a ship's book, a probate file, a church register — and each piece ends with the list of what it rests on, so you can go and look yourself.

Where we do not know something, the pages say so. That includes the two places where the chain of descent is still an inference rather than a proof, and the several places where a date everybody repeats turns out to have no record behind it at all. That is not a weakness in the research. It is the research.

⚑ And none of it takes anything away from the notes. Somebody has to write the first version before anyone can check it — has to care enough to sit down and put the names on paper while he still remembered them. K.O. Schmidt wrote the first version. Everything here is the checking.

Everything below is drawn from the documents themselves and written to be read straight through. The evidence sits in a short section at the end of each piece, so you can check anything without it interrupting the story.

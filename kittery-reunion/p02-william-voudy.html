William Voudy

York, Maine · about 1778 – after 1868

There is no record of his birth, and there never was one.

That is not a search failure. William Voudy was born into a hole in the record, and we know exactly how the hole was made. His family lived nine miles out to sea on the Isles of Shoals, where the Reverend John Tucke had faithfully entered every baptism since 1729 — including those of William's older brothers and sisters. That register closes in 1774. His father Edward Voudy was still serving as a Gosport town officer on Star Island on 11 March 1775, five weeks before Lexington. Within the year the Isles were evacuated and the congregation scattered.

William was born about 1778, to a refugee family, in the three years after the institution that would have written him down ceased to exist. The York town book has been searched under eight spellings. He is not in it, and he never was.

What is missing is a birth date. Not a parent.

His mother and father could not write their names.

On 10 May 1803 Edward Voudy sold his son a piece of land for a hundred and forty dollars, and the signature block of that deed is the plainest thing in this whole story: "Edward H. Voudy" — his mark. "Sarah ✗ Voudy" — her mark. Neither of them could sign.

The son they were conveying to is already styled, in the body of the deed, William Voudy of York, mariner.

That October he went to be married, and the record fails him again.

On 6 October 1803 the York town clerk entered: "William Voudy and Dorcas [Booker] both of York intend marriage." Beneath it he made a tick, and left the rest of the line blank.

⚑ That blank is worth pausing on, because the same clerk on the same page was doing better for other people. Two entries above the Voudys, for a couple whose intention was entered ten days earlier, he wrote across the line in his smaller hand: married November 1803, by — and named the man who performed it. He recorded the wedding date and the officiant for the neighbours. For William Voudy and Dorcas Booker he recorded nothing.

No marriage record for them has ever been found. What we have is the intention, and fifty-five years of two people living as husband and wife in the public record.

In 1811 his mother made her will and put everything in his hands. Sarah Voudy of York, widow, left one dollar each to her daughters Rachel and Lydia, her wearing apparel to Polly, and the whole of the rest — real and personal — to her son William, whom she named sole executor. She was dead within two years.

And then he did something neither of his parents could have done. On 2 April 1816 he sold that house lot to James Newbury of York, calker, for ninety dollars, and wrote his family's history into the deed:

…being the same that was purchased by my grandmother Rachel Varrill of Giles Scot, and left me by will of my mother Sally Voudy.

Three generations, named, in a recorded public instrument, by a man whose parents made crosses. That single sentence is why this family can trace the Varrell and Sadler line at all.

In 1817 he became the man with money. On the seventeenth of November his father-in-law Aaron Booker mortgaged him the whole of a nine-acre farm on the south side of York River — every acre, the buildings, down to the cove that separates Harmon's Island from the mainland — for forty-three dollars and thirteen cents. Aaron signed it Aaron ✗ Booker.

Thirteen years later he was the man who needed it. On 24 May 1830 he mortgaged his own dwelling house and a lot of fifteen square rods for eighty-one dollars. The deed locates the Voudy homestead by its neighbours — Parrott, Joseph Kingsbury, James Donnell — which is how we know where he actually lived.

On 28 March 1837 the two men appear on one sheet of paper.

A neighbour's widow needed someone to swear she had been a wife, and Aaron Booker, aged eighty-four, gave the deposition. At the foot of it, on the right, is Aaron ✗ Booker. On the left, in a clean and confident hand, is the witness: William Voudy.

(The machine transcript renders that signature "Williams his Nancy." It does not say that. The image, read at magnification, says William Voudy — the capital V and the looped terminal y this project knows from twenty York deeds.)

Three years later the old man moved in. The printed federal census of Revolutionary pensioners for 1 June 1840 has three columns, and the line reads: Aaron Booker — 89 — William Voudy.

The farm went to his son, and he kept the use of it. He conveyed the nine acres to Reuben Voudy, mariner, for two hundred dollars — reserving to himself "and to my wife Dorcas Voudy during my life and her life" the complete possession, improvement and profits of the place, "the same as if this deed had not been made." The deed was executed and acknowledged on 11 April 1848 and not put on record until 5 September 1868 — one instrument, not two — and it describes the land as "the same that was formerly owned by Aaron Booker dec.d." ⭐ Because the fee passes only "after our decease", the act of recording it in September 1868 is itself evidence that Dorcas was still living that month.

⭐ That 1868 deed is the single most important document this family owns, and not for the land. It is the only instrument anywhere that names Aaron Booker and Dorcas Voudy together — a woman holding a life estate in the farm of the man we believe was her father.

The census of 1850 shows the household at its fullest: William Voudy, 70, fisherman, four hundred dollars of real estate; Dorcas, 64; their married son Reuben, 25, farmer, with his wife Sarah and their baby George.

In 1854 he went to court as a grandfather. "I William Vondy of York" petitioned the York County Probate Court on 4 February, representing that his daughter Paulina M. Whittier had died on 11 February 1853, "leaving two children Mary Gertrude now aged eight years, and Arethusa aged six." Their father, he told the court, was "absent in California." It was 1854; the goldfields had taken him and the girls needed a guardian. The court appointed one.

By 1860 there was nothing left to count. On 24 July the enumerator found William Voudy, 82, and Dorcas, 64, living alone. No occupation. No real estate. No personal estate. Nothing in any column. Two doors down the road, in dwelling 567, was their son William Voudy Junior, 53, fisherman.

And still they were there in September 1868, both of them, signing away the fee of the nine acres and keeping the use of it — he about ninety years old, she about eighty-two.

After that the record lets him go as quietly as it took him up. Maine kept no statewide death registration until 1892. No probate was opened for him; a search of his son's later deeds for an heirs' partition — the kind of document that names every surviving child — found a routine fifty-dollar sale and no division at all. No death date, no burial place, no stone.

He entered the world in a gap in the record and left through another one. In between, this man whose mother and father could not write their own names put his signature on deed after deed, executed his mother's will, witnessed his father-in-law's oath, and told a probate judge where his granddaughters' father had gone.

He is the reason we can see any of them.

What is not established

  • His death. No date, no place, no stone, no probate. He was living in September 1868 and that is the last of him.
  • His marriage. The intention of 6 October 1803 is all there is. The clerk who annotated his neighbours' weddings that same autumn annotated nothing here.
  • Lydia Jane Voudy as his daughter. No document says it. Reuben, William Junior, Paulina and Rachael are documented as his children; Lydia Jane is not — and everything this family knows about the Voudys, Varrells, Sadlers and the Isles of Shoals depends on her being one of them. The Massachusetts death return of 1894, volume 445 page 505, entry 741, has a column block headed Names and birthplace of parents — and it has been read at the image. The clerk left it empty on her line, while the lines immediately above and below carry parents in the same block. The document that should have closed this joint was opened, and it does not close it.
  • Dorcas Booker as Aaron Booker's daughter. The 1868 deed, which puts Aaron's farm and Dorcas's life estate in one sentence, is the strongest evidence there is — and it still states no relationship.
  • The temperance lodge. A William Voudy was installed Worthy Outer Guard of Georgiana Lodge No. 143 at York in February 1867. Senior would have been eighty-nine, Junior fifty-nine. It is probably the son, and it is not claimed for either.

The mark, in position — the blank band beneath an entry

The York Erasures

For ninety years the standard finding aid for York, Maine has told researchers that marriage records were scraped out of the town book with a knife. They were not. The bracketed word [ . . erasure . . ] marks a place where the original carries a small pen mark — and that mark is the clerk's shorthand for a marriage that took place. The minister's own book has since been found, and it supplies the eight missing dates.

§1

What was believed

York's original Book of Intentions of Marriage was transcribed in 1934 by the DAR's York Committee on Genealogical Research. In that typescript, certain intentions are followed not by a marriage date but by a bracketed phrase. The compilers' preface adds an observation:

so far as can be determined, the erasures were made only in cases where the marriage was performed by Rev. Isaac Lyman

DAR York Committee, preface · film 008218634 img 686

This research file read that as physical destruction, and said so without qualification:

That bracketed phrase is the typescript's notation for a place where the original book has been physically scraped with a knife. The marriage date was not omitted. It was deliberately destroyed.

source row B-27

Eight entries touching this family were affected — including Aaron Booker × Anne Ellingwood, 9 October 1778, the Revolutionary ancestor and the line's anchor. A second row was written up as “the proof case for the whole erasure story.” A third put a Voudy woman on a chase list on the strength of it.

The story shaped eight source rows, a research list, and a request to be made in person at the archive. It was also the tidiest available explanation for an awkward absence — which, in hindsight, was the problem.

§2

How the question opened

Not by suspicion. By a different job.

While sweeping the original manuscript film for Booker entries, the full-resolution master of image 180 — the leaf carrying Aaron Booker's intention — was pulled for an unrelated reason: to read the entry text at magnification. With the master in hand, the thing to look at turned out to be the paper, not the words.

There was no scrape. The band beneath the entry is clean and evenly toned; the ruled lines above and below run continuous and crisp across it; there is no roughened patch, no thinning, no ghost of lifted ink. What sits in the band is a tick and a small double-hatch.

That contradicted the claim above — but a row written eighteen hours earlier, from the same image at lower magnification, had already said so:

the entry carries the book's completion marks beneath it, the same paired tick-and-hash the clerk applied to intentions that went through

source row V-215, 19 August 2026

That clause had since been “corrected” on the typescript's authority. The correction had gone the wrong way, and nobody noticed, because the typescript was assumed to be reporting the page.

§3

What that means

The compilers met a small pen mark standing where they expected a marriage date, could not set it in type, and wrote down what they took it to mean.

One thing fell out unlooked-for. Read the residence column: every hatched couple is both-of-York. The un-hatched ones bring in Berwick, Boston, Kittery — couples who would marry elsewhere, whose returns would never reach the York clerk.

The conclusion

Where the clerk wrote the marriage out in words, there is no hatch and no “erasure.” Where he did not, there is a hatch and an “erasure.”

The hatch stands in place of a written-out marriage. It is the marriage record, in shorthand.

The 1739 page also supplied the control the test needed — two marriages we know beyond doubt took place, because their children are entered in the family birth registers:

140. Amos Gowdy and Mercy Booker both of York intend Marriage Entred Dec.r 8. 1739 & Married by the Rev.d M.r Sam.l Moody Dec.r 27. 1739.

film 007596957 img 147 · read at the image

Both written out. Neither hatched. On a page where he records marriages in words, he does not use the mark.

When did the practice change? — and a claim withdrawn

On two openings it looked as though the clerk's practice changed when the minister changed: the 1739 marriages are all by Rev. Samuel Moody, who died in 1747, and the 1778 page sits in Rev. Isaac Lyman's ministry. That was written up here as the explanation of the DAR's own observation.

Measuring six openings instead of two shows it is a U-curve, not a step:

Lyman was settled at York from 1749 until 1810 — through the trough and through the recovery. The 1791 recovery happens with no change of minister at all. So the ministry cannot be the cause, and the sentence claiming it was is withdrawn. It was half a correlation presented as a cause; the collapse really does coincide with Moody's death, and that made it look compelling.

The DAR's observation survives as a description. The notations do fall only in Lyman's years — because a forty-year trough inside a sixty-one-year ministry cannot fall anywhere else. It is a true statement that explains nothing, which is exactly the trap rule 113 was written about.

The better hypothesis: a habit that turns twice in fifty years while the ministry does not is far more likely the town clerk's than the minister's. The book is the clerk's book. The test is an archive question — who held the office of town clerk at York between 1747 and 1795?

The rule that falls out of it

An entry before about 1747, or after about 1791, may be read by its text — if the marriage happened the clerk probably wrote it out. An entry between about 1750 and 1790 must be read by its marks, and the absence of a written marriage in that band means nothing at all.

That band contains, among this project's own people: Aaron Booker × Anne Ellingwood 1778 (hatched), Nicholas Booker × Hannah Young 1753, Jacob Booker jun.r × Lydia Henney 1767, John Farr × Mary Vowdy 1786. For every one of them the marks are the evidence and the silence is not.

Limits of the counts

Both turns are bracketed, not pinned — the collapse falls in 1740×1752, the recovery in 1786×1791. The counts come from machine transcripts and are approximate; they are used only for the shape, which is far larger than the error. The conventional ministry dates are not sourced here.

§5

Test three — trying to break it

A reading that only ever confirms itself is worth nothing. The 1778 page offered one case that could break this one: Thomas Abbot × Ellis Baker, both of York, 1 August 1778 — tick only, no hatch, when every other both-of-York couple on that page is hatched.

The prediction: if the hatch marks a marriage, this couple either did not marry or married outside York — and should leave no further trace in the town's records.

No marriage. No children in the family birth registers. No death. The one both-of-York couple lacking the clerk's mark is the one couple who leave no history at York at all. A prediction was made from a pen mark, and the records agreed with it.

§6

Test four — the minister's own book

Everything above is an argument about one book, made from that book and its transcript. It needed a witness from outside.

There is one, and it has been sitting in Boston. The Congregational Library & Archives holds the First Parish of York's church records, 1749–1829. Behind the baptisms, past fifty leaves of blank paper and parish business, are thirteen pages headed in the minister's own hand:

Marriages consummated by Isaac Lyman

Congregational Library item 38935, image 213 · book page 249 · first entry 11 June 1765

It is his personal list of the marriages he performed — not a parish register of all York marriages. That distinction matters for every argument built on an absence in it, and it is why an absence before June 1765 means nothing: the book does not begin until then, sixteen years into his ministry.

The eight erased entries touching this family were looked up in it, one at a time.

And the converse, from §5: Thomas Abbot — the tick-only man from the same 1778 opening — was searched for across the register and is not in it.

The finding

The 1934 compilers wrote that the erasures fall only where Lyman performed the marriage. That sentence sat in this file for weeks as an unverified assertion by people who had the book in their hands and never said how they knew.

It is true, eight times out of eight — proved from a book they appear never to have used, because it is in Boston and they cite only the town book.

Which completes the reading. The mark is not damage and not suppression. It is the town clerk's note that Mr Lyman had married the couple and sent in the return. The compilers, working from type and not from the hand, met a manuscript mark they could not set, printed [ . . erasure . . ] for it, noticed correctly that it only ever appeared on Lyman's marriages — and said so in their preface without realising they had just explained it.

What this does not do

The eight were chosen because they touch this family. That is a selected sample, and the 1778 opening alone carries six erasure notations of which only one is ours. Twenty unrelated erasures tested the same way would settle it on an unselected sample; that work is outstanding.

Two rows in the research file still describe the town book as physically knife-scraped, which contradicts the clean-paper reading at §2. Those two leaves have not yet been pulled at full resolution. Until they are, the physical question is open on those entries even though the correlation is not.

The converse test rests on one man. It should rest on five.

§7

What follows

For the direct line

Aaron Booker and Anne Ellingwood's entry carries the hatch. It is not a destroyed record and never was — the clerk recorded that they married, and the minister who married them wrote down the day:

Feb.y 16 1779 — Aaron Booker & Anna Elenwood ✓

Congregational Library item 38935, image 217 · book page 253 · read at native resolution

The date was never missing from the world. It was missing from this side of it.

For the book as a whole

The York Book of Intentions has been read as a list of intentions, with eleven catalogued “failure formulas” invented to explain its silences. A large number of those silences are not silences. They are marriages — and for the years 1765 to 1802 the dates are recoverable, one by one, from a book in Boston.

For anyone else using the DAR typescripts

They are the standard finding aid for York. The bracketed notation should be read as “the original carries the clerk's completion mark here” — not as “a record was destroyed here.” And it is a positive signal, not a negative one: it tells you the marriage happened, and it tells you which minister to go looking for.

What was traded

A dramatic story that could not be proved, for eight marriage dates that could — and a key that makes the rest of the book readable. That is a good trade and it should be said plainly.

§8

What is still open

  1. The converse test has not been run

    Answered, 21 August. It has now been run eight times — see §6. Every erased entry touching this family is a marriage in Lyman's register, and the one tick-only couple is not.

    The candidate this row proposed — Richard Keating × Sally Sayward Barrell — is in the register too, on the leaf above Aaron Booker.

  2. The later hand

    Answered, 21 August. One row showed somebody went through the town book afterwards writing dates in — “Dec 14 by Rev Isaac Lyman.” This asked: whose hand, and what was he copying from?

    He was copying from this register. Where he restored a date it agrees with Lyman's book to the day, twice out of two — Nehemiah Booker 14 Dec 1801 and David Moore 13 Mar 1791. And where no later hand ever restored an entry, the register supplies the date anyway.

  3. Twenty erasures that are nothing to do with us

    The eight are a selected sample. Take twenty erased entries at random from the typescript, across several decades, with no connection to this family, and look for each in the register; then twenty un-erased intentions from the same pages. This is now the sharpest open question here, because it is the one a sceptical reader would raise first — and it needs no login and no travel.

  4. The knife-scrape descriptions

    Two rows in the file describe the town book as physically scraped, which the clean-paper reading at §2 contradicts. Those two leaves — film 007596957 images 787 and 807 — have not been pulled at full resolution. Until they are, the physical question is open on those entries.

  5. Who was town clerk at York, 1747–1795?

    The written-out practice turns twice while the ministry does not, so the habit is very likely the clerk's. York's town officers should exist as a printed list. It would explain the curve at §4.

  6. Raking light on the object

    Original page 24, DAR page [91] — if there is spare time at the counter. It would now only confirm what the correlation already shows, so it is no longer a priority.

Method rules this produced

Rule 113

A transcript's editorial notation is a statement about the transcriber, not about the page. Go and look. An explanation that arrives too neatly is the one to check first.

Rule 115

“Every”, “all”, “none”, “always” are claims about a whole page — crop the whole page before you write one. Written after a generalisation from three entries to twenty-three had to be corrected within the hour. A mark that varies is a mark that means something; a claim of uniformity throws the signal away.

Rule 121

Read from the repository's image service, not from its page viewer. A viewer shows you whatever magnification it feels like and will not tell you when you are below the resolution at which a digit can be decided. Reading this register through one produced six misreadings in a single day, every one corrected at full scale — including a target surname silently misfiled as a different one.

Rule 125

Establish what a document's ordinary furniture is before deciding that a particular mark is extraordinary. The question to ask of any striking mark is not “what could this say” but “what does this scribe habitually write in this position.” And when the answer you want and the answer you have coincide, slow down — that coincidence is the least reliable moment in the whole process.
